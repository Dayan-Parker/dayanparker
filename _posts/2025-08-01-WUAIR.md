I founded WashU AI Racing (WUAIR) to compete in the 2026 IMechE Formula Student AI (FSAI) global competition at Silverstone Circuit in the UK. The team has grown to over 30 members — engineers, financial administrators, and sponsorship managers — organized into four specialized software divisions and a business team. We are building a full level-5 autonomous driving system for a prebuilt drive-by-wire electric car capable of exceeding 80 mph.

## The Competition

FSAI takes place each July at the **Silverstone F1 circuit** in the UK. Every team receives the same prebuilt ADS-DV — a Formula Student-sized electric car with drive-by-wire steering, brakes, and throttle — and develops the sensor suite and software stack themselves. No modifications to the chassis or electronics are permitted; the competition advantage comes entirely from software and cleverly chosen hardware.

Scoring covers 1,000 points split across static and dynamic events:

| Event | Points |
|---|---|
| Autonomous Design presentation | 150 |
| Business Plan presentation | 100 |
| Real World AI presentation | 100 |
| Simulation Development presentation | 100 |
| Trackdrive (10 laps, novel track) | 250 |
| Autocross / Sprint (1 lap, novel track) | 100 |
| Skid Pad (figure-eight) | 100 |
| Acceleration (straight-line) | 100 |

In the dynamic events the car navigates an unknown track cold — yellow cones always mark the right boundary, blue the left — building its global map from scratch using SLAM while racing at speed, with no safety driver.

## Team Structure

Running a team this size as a student organization requires real engineering management. I co-founded the team with Amelia Hines, Eugene Joo, and Mario Rodriguez-Montoya, and lead across both the technical and organizational sides — managing recruitment, external sponsorships, and engineering development simultaneously.

**Why agile.** My year at Formula Trinity made one thing clear: a waterfall approach — where requirements are fixed upfront, teams build in parallel, and everything integrates at the end — is fatal for an adaptive system like an autonomous car. Requirements change constantly, environments are unpredictable, and a bug discovered during the final integration week can end a season. WUAIR instead follows an agile philosophy: small, frequent releases of working software, continuous iteration, and a structure that can absorb change.

**Squads and tribes.** I adapted the Spotify Model for our scale. The four software divisions each operate as a self-managed **squad** — a mini-startup of six to eight engineers with a single long-term mission and the autonomy to choose their own development approach:

- **Perception** — visual detection and 3D localization of track cones
- **State Estimation** — cone mapping and vehicle localization via Fast SLAM
- **Path Planning** — optimal race line and velocity profile computation
- **Control** — motor and steering actuation to execute the planned trajectory

The four squads are grouped into two **tribes** to enforce cross-team communication: Perception and State Estimation (both concerned with cone positioning) form one tribe, while Path Planning and Control (both concerned with driving the car) form the other. Squad leads are technical leads, not pure administrators — their role is to guide development methodology while actively contributing code.

**Development workflow.** Each squad runs a hybrid **Scrum-Kanban** process tracked on taiga.io: Scrum-style sprints define short, shippable features; Kanban-style WIP limits prevent bottlenecks and keep work visible to the whole squad. Because members meet at most twice a week, squads hold short virtual scrums between in-person sessions to stay aligned without the overhead of full meetings. We also run **hack days** — dedicated time for members to prototype ideas outside the sprint backlog — as a structured outlet for innovation that can feed back into the roadmap.

**Onboarding.** Student clubs have constant member turnover, so onboarding has to be fast. Inspired by Meta's engineering bootcamp, new members work through a **two-week case study** — a skeletal implementation of the full autonomous stack with pieces missing and automated unit tests to verify their solutions. This gets them past the initial ROS and Git learning curve and exposed to every squad before they choose one to join, typically making their first real code contribution within weeks of joining.

The goal, as I described it for the WashU Engineering news feature, is to build "rigorously engineered, complex software" while also maintaining the collaborative, community-driven culture that makes the team a place people genuinely want to be.

The full autonomous stack covers every layer of the robotics pipeline. The sensor suite pairs an **OAK-D Pro PoE** stereo RGB camera with a **HESAI Pandar XT32** scanning LiDAR, GPS, and an IMU. All modules communicate through **ROS2** and are validated in the open-source **EUFS Gazebo simulator** before on-car testing. Processing runs on an **NVIDIA Jetson Nano**.

## Perception Research

My primary technical contribution to the team is the perception stack — the first stage of the pipeline and the one every other module depends on. I developed a **LiDAR/Camera sensor fusion** architecture with Eugene Joo, under the supervision of **Professor Yiannis Kantaros**.

The core challenge: YOLO gives accurate visual detections in 2D image space, but cannot tell you *where* in 3D space a cone is. LiDAR gives centimeter-accurate 3D point clouds but cannot classify objects. Fusing the two gives both.

### Pipeline

**1. Object Detection** — We use **YOLOv8-Nano** for its speed and small footprint, trained on the FOSCO (Formula Student Objects in Context) dataset. After 150 epochs the model achieves a **mAP@50 of ~0.76** for real-time cone detection. Images are optionally downsampled at test time to reduce inference latency.

**2. LiDAR Filtering** — The HESAI Pandar XT32 produces **640,000 points per rotation** at 10 Hz with 1 cm spatial accuracy. That density is computationally expensive, so we filter aggressively:
- Z-axis threshold removes ground and sky returns
- Distance cutoff at **30 meters** removes far-field noise where cone resolution is too low to be useful

**3. LiDAR-Camera Projection** — To fuse the 3D point cloud with 2D YOLO bounding boxes, we project each LiDAR point into image coordinates. First the point is transformed from LiDAR frame to camera frame using the extrinsic calibration matrix (rotation R and translation T between sensors):

$$\begin{bmatrix} X_C \\ Y_C \\ Z_C \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} R & T \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} X_L \\ Y_L \\ Z_L \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

The resulting camera-frame point $$P_C(X_C, Y_C, Z_C)$$ is then projected onto the 2D image plane using the OAK-D's intrinsic matrix $$K$$, which encodes the sensor's focal lengths and optical center $$(c_x, c_y)$$ in pixels:

$$\begin{bmatrix} u \cdot Z_C \\ v \cdot Z_C \\ Z_C \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 448.13 & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & 448.13 & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} X_C \\ Y_C \\ Z_C \end{bmatrix}$$

Dividing through by the depth $$Z_C$$ gives the final pixel coordinates:

$$u = \frac{448.13 \cdot X_C}{Z_C} + c_x, \quad v = \frac{448.13 \cdot Y_C}{Z_C} + c_y$$

Any LiDAR point whose projected pixel coordinates fall inside a YOLO bounding box becomes part of that cone's 3D Region of Interest.

**4. DBSCAN Clustering** — To extract the precise centroid of each cone from its ROI point cloud, we apply **DBSCAN** (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise). DBSCAN's density-based approach makes it resilient to the noise and outliers present in real-world LiDAR returns. Each cone is published with a full **covariance matrix** — the uncertainty estimate consumed directly by the downstream SLAM algorithm to weight each detection appropriately when building the global map.

**5. ROS2 Integration** — The full pipeline runs as a ROS2 node that triggers on incoming camera and LiDAR data and publishes two arrays (yellow cones, blue cones) for downstream modules to consume.

### Results

Tested in the EUFS simulator over full laps at the mapping speed of up to **7 m/s**:
- Near **100% cone detection rate**
- Full stack throughput of **~43 Hz** — well above the real-time threshold for safe navigation

## Looking Ahead

The competition is July 2026 at Silverstone. The perception stack is being prepared for deployment on the physical vehicle. The broader team is integrating all four software divisions — SLAM, path planning, control — toward a full autonomous run on the ADS-DV car.
